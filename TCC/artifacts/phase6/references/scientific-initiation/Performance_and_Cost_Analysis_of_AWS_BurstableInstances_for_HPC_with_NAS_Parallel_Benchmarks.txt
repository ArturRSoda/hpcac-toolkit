Performance and Cost Analysis of AWS Burstable
Instances for HPC with NAS Parallel Benchmarks

Abstract—Traditional High Performance Computing (HPC)
typically requires expensive and complex physical infrastruc-
tures. With the advancement of cloud computing, providers
such as Amazon Web Services (AWS) have begun to offer
more economically viable alternatives, such as burstable and
spot instances. However, selecting the appropriate resources
remains challenging due to performance variability and the costs
associated with different workload profiles. In this context, this
work evaluates the feasibility of using burstable instances for
HPC. To this end, an in-depth study was carried out using AWS
on-demand burstable and non-burstable instances, evaluating
their performance and cost under different HPC application
scenarios. Three main sets of experiments were conducted: (1)
comparative evaluation on homogeneous clusters of up to 4 nodes
using NAS Parallel Benchmarks (NPB), contrasting burstable and
similar non-burstable instances; (2) scalability analysis on larger
clusters (4–16 nodes) with a broader variety of NPB applications;
(3) isolated measurement of the impact of network bandwidth
using an MPI microkernel benchmark. The results show that
the ideal instance type depends on the workload profile: non-
burstable instances provide the best cost-benefit for intensive
and distributed workloads, while burstable standard may be
more economical for light and short workloads, and burstable
unlimited ensures constant performance at a higher cost for long-
running workloads.
Index Terms—high performance computing, cloud computing,
NAS parallel benchmarks, burstable instances
I. INTRODUCTION
High Performance Computing (HPC) plays a fundamental
role in supporting scientific discovery, engineering simula-
tions, and large-scale data analysis applications that require
massive computational resources. Traditionally, HPC work-
loads have been executed on dedicated supercomputers or
large-scale on-premises clusters. However, in recent years, the
emergence and rapid growth of cloud computing have reshaped
this landscape by introducing elasticity, flexibility, and the
ability to provision computational resources on demand [1].
These capabilities have made Cloud Computing an attractive
alternative or complement to traditional HPC infrastructures,
particularly in environments where scalability and cost effi-
ciency are critical.
The convergence of HPC and Cloud Computing has gained
significant attention, as researchers and practitioners explore
how the elasticity of cloud infrastructures can support the de-
manding performance requirements of HPC workloads. While
cloud providers have traditionally targeted web services, data
storage, and business applications [2], recent advancements
have led to specialized services tailored for computationally
intensive tasks. This convergence raises important questions
about how to balance performance, cost, and resource avail-
ability when executing HPC applications in the cloud. In par-
ticular, the pay-as-you-go pricing models and heterogeneous
resource types offered by commercial providers present both
opportunities and challenges for the HPC community. As a
result, Cloud Computing has become not only a viable option
but also an increasingly strategic platform for addressing the
computational demands of modern scientific and industrial
applications.
Among the different cloud instance models, Amazon Web
Services (AWS) provides a representative portfolio that in-
cludes on-demand and spot pricing models, which can be ap-
plied to various instance types including burstable performance
instances. On-demand pricing guarantees stable availability
and continuous access, but at a relatively high hourly cost.
Spot pricing, on the other hand, offers significant discounts by
leveraging unused capacity, but instances may be interrupted at
any moment by the cloud provider, posing challenges for long-
running HPC jobs. Burstable performance instances represent
a distinct instance type that can use either pricing model: they
provide baseline performance levels while allowing workloads
to temporarily “burst” above that baseline by consuming
accumulated CPU credits. This makes them attractive for cost-
sensitive users, though their variable performance model intro-
duces uncertainty when applied to computationally intensive
workloads. Each pricing model and instance type presents
trade-offs between cost, reliability, and performance, which
must be carefully evaluated when planning HPC workloads in
the cloud [3].
A key open question is whether burstable instances can be
effectively leveraged for HPC workloads. Their lower hourly
rates present clear cost advantages, but the credit-based CPU
model may severely degrade performance for long-running
or highly parallel applications. For HPC environments where
both execution time and monetary cost are critical factors,
it is essential to evaluate whether burstable instances can
provide a feasible balance between efficiency and affordability.
Understanding these trade-offs is particularly relevant for
researchers, institutions, and organizations operating under
constrained budgets. Moreover, the possibility of combining


different instance types (e.g., using burstable instances as tem-
porary replacements for interrupted spot instances) introduces
opportunities for cost optimization and fault tolerance that
merit systematic investigation.
In this paper, we address this problem by evaluating the
performance and cost of burstable performance instances in the
context of HPC applications, comparing them with traditional
non-burstable on-demand instances. Using the NAS Parallel
Benchmarks (NPB) as representative workloads [4], we ana-
lyze how burstable instances behave under different execution
conditions, and we assess their cost-effectiveness in various
cluster configurations. Our contributions are the following:
• We analyze the performance behavior of burstable in-
stances in comparison to non-burstable instances across
multiple HPC workloads;
• We investigate the impact of the number of cluster nodes
on both execution time and overall cost; and
• We study the influence of network bandwidth of burstable
instances on the application performance.
To systematically investigate the performance–cost trade-
offs, our study is structured around three complementary ex-
perimental phases, each designed to answer a critical question.
The first phase serves as a foundational analysis, providing
a direct comparison of burstable and non-burstable instances
under controlled, small-scale conditions. Its primary impor-
tance lies in establishing the fundamental performance and
cost differences inherent to the instance types. Building on
this, the second phase examines scalability, which is cru-
cial for understanding how these initial findings translate to
more realistic, larger-scale HPC deployments, where inter-
node communication becomes a dominant factor. Finally,
the third phase employs a micro-benchmark to isolate the
impact of network bandwidth from other architectural vari-
ables, providing a causal explanation for the performance gaps
observed and quantifying the specific role of networking in
the overall performance equation. Together, this multi-phase
approach provides a comprehensive analysis, moving from
baseline comparison to scaled analysis and finally to a specific
investigation.
This paper is organized as follows. Section II introduces
the necessary background, including the paradigm of HPC
in the cloud, the characteristics of AWS pricing models (on-
demand and spot) and instance types (including burstable
performance instances), the HPC@Cloud provisioning tool,
and the NAS Parallel Benchmarks used in our evaluation.
Section III describes the experimental methodology, detailing
the instance types under study, the selected NPB workloads,
and the design of three core experiments for performance
assessment. Section IV presents and analyzes the results,
with comparative performance graphs and cost-effectiveness
evaluations. Finally, Section VI summarizes the main findings,
highlights their implications for selecting suitable instance
types according to workload profiles, and discusses promising
directions for future research, including intelligent strategies
for dynamic resource provisioning.
II. BACKGROUND
A. HPC and Cloud Convergence
High Performance Computing (HPC) traditionally refers
to the use of on-premise computer clusters for executing
large-scale computations in parallel. These clusters, commonly
known as supercomputers, enable the collaborative effort of
millions of processing elements to solve problems that would
be infeasible on a single machine within a reasonable time-
frame. Thus, parallelization became a fundamental approach
to achieving computational efficiency in HPC.
HPC applications are often tailored to the underlying
hardware, exploiting specialized processor instructions and
accelerators to maximize performance. At the programming
level, several parallel programming models and APIs have
been developed to exploit both shared-memory and distributed-
memory systems. One of the most widely adopted models
for shared-memory architectures is OpenMP, which provides a
high-level programming interface for C, C++, and Fortran, us-
ing compiler directives to generate parallel code. OpenMP in-
cludes directives for parallelizing loops, synchronizing threads,
and managing task dependencies. Since OpenMP assumes a
shared-memory system, it is mainly used for intra-node paral-
lelism in HPC clusters. For inter-node parallelization across
distributed-memory systems, the Message Passing Interface
(MPI) has become the de facto standard. It defines a portable
and language-independent programming model for distributed
computing. Multiple open-source and proprietary implemen-
tations of MPI exist, with OpenMPI, MPICH, and MVAPICH
being among the most widely used. These implementations
act as middleware, providing abstractions for process manage-
ment, synchronization, and communication. MPI supports both
point-to-point communications (between specific processes)
and collective communications (among groups of processes).
Although MPI has long been the cornerstone of distributed
HPC applications, it is frequently combined with shared-
memory programming models such as OpenMP to fully ex-
ploit the hierarchical structure of modern HPC clusters. In this
hybrid approach, OpenMP handles fine-grained parallelism
within a node, while MPI coordinates communication and
workload distribution across nodes, enabling efficient use of
large-scale supercomputing infrastructures.
Cloud Computing, on the other hand, has emerged as a
dominant paradigm for delivering computing resources as on-
demand services over the Internet. Public clouds, in par-
ticular, are designed to be accessible to any user without
requiring long-term contracts or direct negotiation with the
provider. Their services are traditionally classified into three
service models: Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), Platform
as a Service (PaaS), and Software as a Service (SaaS). In
the IaaS model, providers expose APIs that enable users to
provision and manage compute infrastructure, including low-
level components such as networking, storage, and backups.
Users can select the computing capacity to be rented in terms
of virtual CPUs (vCPUs), along with additional configurations
such as hypervisor types, operating systems, and accelerators.


PaaS services abstract runtime, middleware, and development
tools, allowing users to create and deploy applications without
managing the underlying infrastructure. Finally, SaaS provides
fully managed, ready-to-use software applications exposed
through APIs, which can be directly integrated into user
workflows.
The convergence of HPC and cloud computing reflects a
balance between two traditionally distinct paradigms: the strict
performance and low-latency requirements of scientific work-
loads and the flexibility, resilience, and pay-as-you-go pricing
model of cloud services. For researchers and organizations
with limited budgets, the IaaS model eliminates the need
for costly on-premise supercomputers, lowering both capital
investment and technical entry barriers to HPC. At the same
time, cloud platforms have matured to support rapid provi-
sioning and elastic scaling of virtualized resources, enabling
infrastructures to adapt dynamically to workload demands.
While enterprise applications often prioritize resiliency and
fault tolerance, HPC workloads demand the opposite, concen-
trating resources in close proximity to minimize communica-
tion latency. Bridging these differing requirements highlights
both the challenges and the opportunities of bringing HPC into
the cloud, motivating new strategies that combine performance
efficiency with the economic and operational advantages of
cloud infrastructures.
More recently, to support high-performance workloads,
cloud providers have offered specialized HPC-oriented ser-

B. Cloud Compute and Pricing Models
Cloud providers operate fleets of servers distributed across
multiple data centers and offer users access to computing
resources in the form of Virtual Machines (VMs). A VM is a
virtual instance allocated over a shared physical infrastructure
and represents the most common and cost-effective option in
Cloud Computing. Providers expose a wide variety of instance
types that differ in characteristics such as CPU architecture,
number of vCPUs, memory capacity, storage configuration,
and network bandwidth. This diversity enables customers to
select instances that best match the requirements of their
applications.
Among public cloud providers, Amazon Web Services
(AWS) is one of the most widely adopted, maintaining a
global infrastructure of data centers that deliver computational
resources to users. AWS offers two primary pricing models
that can be applied to various instance types:
On-demand pricing. This follows a “pay-as-you-go” model
in which users are billed per second of compute time
with no long-term commitments. On-demand instances
guarantee stable and predictable availability, making them
suitable for a broad range of workloads.
Spot pricing. This leverages unused cloud capacity at sig-
nificantly reduced prices, often up to 90% cheaper than
on-demand equivalents, while providing identical perfor-
mance characteristics. However, spot instances can be
reclaimed by AWS with little notice whenever demand for
capacity increases. Consequently, users must design their
applications with fault tolerance in mind, ensuring data
persistence and recovery mechanisms in case of instance
termination.
These pricing models can be applied to different instance
families, each optimized for specific workload characteristics.
Burstable instances are a family of instance types optimized
for workloads with variable CPU demand. They provide a
baseline performance level but can temporarily exceed this
baseline using a system of CPU credits. Credits are accumu-
lated whenever the instance operates below baseline usage and
are spent to enable short-term performance bursts. Each credit
typically corresponds to one vCPU running at full utilization
for one minute.
AWS defines two operating modes for burstable instances:
Standard Mode: once CPU credits are exhausted, the in-
stance is throttled down to its baseline performance,
which can cause severe slowdowns in long-running or
compute-intensive workloads; and
Unlimited Mode: This mode allows an instance to contin-
uously burst above its baseline even after credits are
exhausted, but it incurs an additional hourly charge for
every vCPU-minute consumed beyond the earned cred-
its, ensuring stable performance at a potentially higher
financial cost.
AWS has released several families of burstable instances,
which share the credit-based CPU model but differ in their
underlying processor architecture and generation. Key families
include the legacy T2 (previous generation, Intel processors),
T3 and T3a (current generation with Intel and AMD proces-
sors, respectively), and the more recent T4g (featuring AWS’s
Graviton2 ARM-based processors). These families offer a
range of options that balance cost and processor type.
In this paper, we focus on the performance and cost eval-
uation of instances under the on-demand pricing model (both
burstable and non-burstable families), since our target HPC
applications are not fault-tolerant and are therefore unsuitable
for spot pricing.
C. NAS Parallel Benchmarks
The NAS Parallel Benchmarks (NPB)1 is a suite of par-
allel benchmark programs designed by the NASA Advanced
Supercomputing (NAS) Division [4]. By measuring various
aspects of performance, such as computational speed and
efficiency, NPB provides a standardized way for researchers
and developers to assess the capabilities of parallel computing
systems, including clusters, supercomputers, and distributed
1https://www.nas.nasa.gov/software/npb.html


memory architectures. It focuses on various aspects of par-
allel computing, including computation, communication, and
memory access patterns.
NPB kernels are derived from Computational Fluid Dynam-
ics (CFD) applications, representing real-world aerospace and
engineering applications. Reference implementations of the
benchmarks are provided by NASA using different parallel
programming libraries such as OpenMP and Message Passing
Interface (MPI). In this paper, we used the most recent
reference MPI implementation (version 3.4.2), which includes
five kernels (two implemented in C and three in Fortran).
Input problem sizes in NPB are predefined and indicated
as different classes. The standard sizes consist of six classes
(S, W, A, B, C, and D): (i) S and W are designed for quick
test purposes; (ii) A, B, and C are the standard inputs, with
each class experiencing approximately a four-fold increase in
size compared to the previous one; and (iii) D, E and F are
the large inputs, with each class experiencing approximately
a sixteen-fold increase in size compared to the previous one.
A brief description of the kernels considered in this paper
is given below:
• Integer Sort (IS): It performs an integer sorting (bucket
sort) among a sparse set of numbers, which simulates
an important computation for particle-in-cell applications.
This kernel simulates and measures integer computation
and data communication capabilities.
• Embarrassingly Parallel (EP): It is an embarrassingly
parallel benchmark that generates pairs of Gaussian ran-
dom deviates according to a specific scheme. This kernel
features negligible communication overhead.
• Conjugate Gradient (CG): It employs a Conjugate Gra-
dient method to compute an approximation to the smallest
eigenvalue of a large, sparse, unstructured matrix. This
kernel tests unstructured grid computations and commu-
nications by using a matrix with randomly generated
locations of entries. It features irregular memory access
and communication patterns.
• Multi-Grid (MG): It uses a V-cycle MultiGrid method to
compute the solution of the 3D scalar Poisson equation.
The algorithm works continuously on a set of grids that
are made between coarse and fine. It tests both short and
long-distance data movements.
• Discrete 3D Fast Fourier Transform (FT): It contains
the computational kernel of a 3D Fast Fourier Transform
(FFT)-based spectral method. FT performs three one-
dimensional (1D) FFTs, one for each dimension, and
features long-distance communications.
• Lower-Upper Symmetric Gauss-Seidel (LU): It imple-
ments a Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) applica-
tion that numerically solves a set of nonlinear Partial Dif-
ferential Equations (PDEs) using an approximate Lower-
Upper (LU) factorization scheme. The equations being
solved are a simplified model of unsteady, compressible
fluid flow in three spatial dimensions, which makes
the kernel representative of real-world CFD applications
widely used in engineering and scientific domains.
This combination of benchmarks provides a comprehensive
assessment of AWS instances’ performance, covering tightly
coupled, latency-sensitive workloads as well as highly paral-
lelizable workloads limited by CPU processing power.
III. METHODOLOGY
To assess whether AWS burstable instances can be effec-
tively employed for HPC workloads, we executed the NPB ap-
plications described in Section II-C in both burstable and non-
burstable instances. Our methodology is structured into three
distinct experimental phases, each designed to progressively
build a comprehensive understanding of the trade-offs of using
AWS burstable instances for HPC. The first phase establishes
a direct performance and cost baseline on small clusters,
representing the foundational contribution of our study. The
second phase assesses how these findings translate to larger-
scale scenarios, providing critical insights into the impact of
scalability. The third and final phase uses a targeted micro-
benchmark to isolate and quantify the influence of network
bandwidth, a key variable identified in the preceding analyzes.
This multi-stage approach ensures a thorough evaluation by
focusing on two primary metrics across all phases: execution
time and monetary cost (in USD).
At the time of this study, AWS burstable instances consisted
of the T4g, T3a, and T3 families, along with the legacy
T2 family. Since these instances are not available under the
usual on-demand pricing model, a fair comparison required
selecting instances from alternative non-burstable families
with equivalent hardware specifications. After examining the
available options, we identified that the T3 (burstable) and
M5 (non-burstable) families utilize the Intel Xeon Cascade
Lake 8259CL processor running at 3.1GHz. Considering ser-
vice limits and cost constraints, we selected the 2xlarge
variant from each family, which provides 8 virtual CPUs (4
physical cores). Detailed hardware specifications are provided
in Table I. It is worth noting, however, that the T3 and M5
families differ in their maximum network bandwidth. Conse-
quently, we anticipated potential performance degradation in
communication-intensive workloads when deploying clusters
of T3 instances.
We used the HPC@Cloud Toolkit2 [5] to provision ho-
mogeneous cluster infrastructures with 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16
nodes, as well as to manage application execution on AWS.
The HPC@Cloud Toolkit is an open-source framework that
simplifies the deployment and management of HPC workloads
in cloud environments. It offers a collection of tools and scripts
that automate the provisioning of cloud resources, enabling
users to configure and manage complex HPC clusters on
platforms such as Amazon Web Services (AWS) with minimal
effort. The toolkit supports a variety of HPC configurations,
allowing users to define instance types, networking settings,
and storage options to suit the specific requirements of their
workloads. By abstracting the intricacies of resource orchestra-
tion, HPC@Cloud accelerates the process of establishing and

IV. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
The experimental evaluation highlights the complex trade-
offs between performance and costs when executing HPC
workloads on AWS using burstable and non-burstable in-
stances. Across the three experimental sets, consistent patterns
emerged that reveal both the strengths and limitations of each
instance type in different workload scenarios.
A. Burstable vs. Non-Burstable Instances
In this first experiment, we restricted the analysis to two
NAS Parallel Benchmarks applications executed with Class
E: EP and LU. This choice was deliberate, as they represent
two opposite workload profiles: EP is short-lived and almost
free of communication, whereas LU is long-lived and highly
communication-intensive.
The comparison between non-burstable (m5.2xlarge)
and burstable (t3.2xlarge) in clusters of up to 4 nodes
revealed two distinct behaviors. For fast execution and commu-
nication light workloads (EP), the performance (Figure 1) and
monetary costs (Figure 1) were similar, showing that burstable
instances can sustain near non-burstable performance as long
as CPU credits are available.
Conversely, in communication-intensive applications such
as LU, the difference was significant, and burstable instances
in standard mode showed up to three times lower performance
than the non-burstable ones. This gap can be explained by two
main factors. First, the higher baseline network bandwidth
of non-burstable instances played a crucial role in handling
the frequent synchronizations required by LU, ensuring lower
communication overhead. Second, the prolonged runtime of
LU quickly exhausted the CPU credits available in burstable
standard instances, thereby limiting CPU performance to base-
line capacity and causing a marked degradation in execution
time. The burstable in unlimited mode partially mitigated this
issue, sustaining performance at the cost of additional charges
once the CPU credits were exhausted. This highlights a key
trade-off: while unlimited mode can prevent unpredictable
slowdowns, it undermines the economic advantage of burstable
instances in longer workloads.

B. Performance Scalability
The second experiment extended the analysis by increasing
the cluster size to 4, 8, and 16 nodes, while also broadening
the set of benchmarks to include LU, EP, MG, CG, IS, and
FT. For this experiment, Class D of the NPB applications was
selected, since the memory available in the m5.2xlarge
and t3.2xlarge instances (GiB) was insufficient to execute
some of the applications with Class E.
The results, illustrated in Figures 2 and 3, demonstrate
that scalability amplifies the inherent performance differences
between instance types. While increasing the number of nodes
reduced execution time for all configurations, the correspond-
ing cost increased due to the higher number of billed instances.
For highly parallel kernels (EP, IS, MG), the non-burstable
cluster was consistently faster but not drastically so. However,
for synchronization-heavy kernels such as LU, CG, and FT, the
performance gap widened with node count, as communication
overhead accumulated and the network limitations of burstable
instances became more apparent. This shows that burstable in-
stances are penalized as communication requirements increase.
When considering performance and cost together, non-
burstable instances maintained the best overall results. The
difference from burstable instances grew with cluster size,
likely due to the stronger impact of network bandwidth as
communication demands increased. Burstable in unlimited
mode achieved intermediate behavior, often outperforming
burstable standard in both time and cost for longer workloads,
since the extra charges for CPU credits were offset by more
stable performance. The burstable instances in standard mode
were only competitive in short scenarios such as EP and
IS, where the initial CPU credits were not exhausted and
costs remained low. However, in longer executions, it suffered
severe performance drops and relative cost increases due to its
baseline capacity limit after credit depletion.
C. Communication Overhead
The third experiment aimed to isolate the impact of network
bandwidth from other architectural factors. For this purpose,
a simple MPI microkernel was implemented and executed on
clusters consisting of 4, 8 and 16 burstable unlimited and non-
burstable instances. The microkernel executes a CPU-intensive
loop with a fixed number of iterations in each MPI process,
thereby enabling the evaluation of whether network band-
width alone explains the performance gap observed between
burstable unlimited and non-burstable instances.
As shown in Figure 4, the performance gap between
burstable unlimited and non-burstable decreased considerably
under these conditions. Cost differences, which had reached
around 40% in the NPB benchmarks, dropped to 10%, while
performance differences were reduced from 50% to 20%.
These results confirm that bandwidth is indeed a dominant
factor behind non-burstable advantage, but not the only one.
The persistence of a smaller gap suggests that CPU credit
management, such as how AWS internally tracks and applies
credits, may be a contributing factor. However, other aspects
may also play a role, including differences in virtualization
overhead, memory latency, or CPU scheduling policies applied
to burstable instances. Such factors introduce subtle overheads
that remain relevant even in less communication-intensive
workloads, and must be considered when evaluating burstable
instances for HPC scenarios with fluctuating CPU demands.

Wang et al. [6] conducted one of the first studies inves-

Mandal et al. [7] addressed dynamic optimization of
burstable instances using online algorithms that migrate work-
loads between different baseline configurations. Their ap-
proach focuses on computational workloads with variable CPU
demands, developing techniques to minimize surplus charges
while maximizing credit utilization. The authors demonstrated
that online algorithms can achieve costs comparable to future-
aware algorithms without requiring prior knowledge of CPU
utilization patterns.
Drummond et al. [8] explored burstable instances for web
scraping frameworks, demonstrating cost effectiveness for data
extraction workloads. Their framework combines fixed and
temporary burstable VMs in mixed clusters to meet Service
Level Agreements (SLAs) while minimizing costs. They de-
veloped queue-based and credit-based allocation strategies,
achieving cost reductions of up to 74% compared to fixed-
size clusters while maintaining SLA compliance.
Our work distinguishes itself by providing a systematic
evaluation of burstable instances specifically for HPC work-
loads using standardized benchmarks. While previous studies
explored optimization techniques and alternative application
domains, our research directly addresses whether burstable
instances can serve as viable alternatives to non-burstable
instances for parallel scientific computing, considering perfor-


mance and cost implications across varying cluster scales and
communication patterns.
VI. CONCLUSION
The primary objective of this work was to evaluate whether
AWS burstable instances can be effectively leveraged for HPC
in terms of both performance and monetary cost. To achieve
this, we employed a three-pronged experimental approach
that first established a baseline comparison, then examined
performance under scalability, and finally isolated the impact
of key architectural factors. This structured investigation led
to a clear conclusion: the most suitable instance type is
intrinsically linked to the workload’s communication intensity
and execution duration.
Our analysis shows that non-burstable instances are the
superior option for sustained, communication-intensive HPC
applications. Their guaranteed network bandwidth and consis-
tent compute performance make them both faster and more
cost-effective for tightly coupled workloads such as LU and
FT, particularly as cluster size increases. In these cases, the
higher hourly cost of non-burstable instances is consistently
offset by substantially reduced execution times, yielding the
most favorable cost–benefit ratio.
In contrast, burstable instances are best suited for short-
lived or embarrassingly parallel tasks such as EP and IS. For
these workloads, the initial CPU credit balance is sufficient to
complete execution before throttling occurs, enabling perfor-
mance comparable to that of non-burstable instances at a lower
hourly rate. However, this advantage disappears for longer or
communication-heavy runs, where credit depletion leads to
severe performance degradation and/or increased relative cost.
The unlimited burstable mode provides an intermediate
solution by preventing throttling and thus ensuring more pre-
dictable performance. While this mode mitigates the instability
of standard burstable instances, it incurs significant financial
overhead due to excess credit charges in prolonged compu-
tations. As a result, although it guarantees stable execution,
it undermines the primary cost-saving rationale of burstable
instances, making it less economical than non-burstable alter-
natives for long-running HPC jobs.
A critical finding from the network isolation experiment
(CPU-bound microkernel) is that the performance disparity
cannot be attributed solely to network bandwidth. The persis-
tence of a measurable performance gap (approximately 20%)
indicates the presence of additional architectural overheads
intrinsic to the burstable instance model, potentially arising
from internal credit management or virtualization mechanisms.
Overall, these results provide a detailed characterization
of burstable and non-burstable instances in HPC contexts,
particularly with respect to communication intensity and work-
load duration. They support more informed decisions when
selecting AWS resources for different HPC profiles. Further-
more, this study establishes a foundation for future research
directions, including the exploration of intelligent dynamic
provisioning strategies. One promising avenue is the design of
fault-tolerance mechanisms [9] capable of integrating spot and
burstable instances within the same cluster. In such a scheme,
burstable instances could remain idle while accumulating
credits and be temporarily activated to replace interrupted spot
instances, thereby ensuring continuity of execution until new
spot resources become available. If viable, this approach could
reduce costs without substantially compromising performance,
and offers an attractive direction for subsequent investigations.
