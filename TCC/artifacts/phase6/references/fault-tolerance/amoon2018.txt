On the design of reactive approach with flexible checkpoint interval 
to tolerate faults in cloud computing systems
Mohammed Amoon1,2   · Nirmeen El‑Bahnasawy2 · Samy Sadi3 · Manar Wagdi2
Received: 22 July 2018 / Accepted: 12 November 2018

Abstract
The likelihood of failures rises in cloud computing systems as a result of their unstable nature. Additionally, the size of 
a cloud computing system varies with time and thus failures become a common incident. Failures have a high impact on 
cloud performance and the expected benefits for both customers and providers. Fault tolerance is an essential challenge 
facing cloud providers in order to mitigate the effects of failures and maintaining the Service Level Agreement (SLA) satis‑
fied. Checkpointing is one of the most known reactive fault tolerance techniques used in distributed computing. However, 
it can incur considerable overheads that depend on the interval of the checkpoint applied and these overheads put down the 
performance of the cloud. In this paper, a reactive fault tolerance approach in the context of checkpointing is proposed and 
evaluated with the aim of getting better performance. The approach depends on applying a flexible interval of the checkpoint 
to reduce overheads. Simulation experiments indicate superior performance of the approach in terms of power consumption, 
response time, monetary cost and cloud capacity.
Keywords  Cloud computing · Fault tolerance · Checkpointing · Checkpoint interval · Cloud capacity
1  Introduction
Cloud computing is the use of computer technology to pro‑
vide different services to clients or customers over the inter‑
net on demand. The main purpose of cloud computing is to 
provide IT services to customers without the high cost of 
installing and maintaining equipment and software applica‑
tions. With cloud computing, any user can access computing 
resources from anywhere as long as a network connection 
is available (Buyya et al. 2009). Cloud computing has many 
advantages and characteristics that can improve the avail‑
ability of resources, save the cost and make users get ser‑
vices and share resources anytime and anywhere (Kumar and 
Goudar 2012; Singh and Jain 2014).
Most cloud applications are executed on remote comput‑
ing nodes, so there is a high probability of failures occur‑
rence that highly influences the performance of the cloud 
(Patel and Singh 2013). With the highly unstable nature of 
clouds, failures become recurrent and not incident. Clouds 
are negatively affected by occurrence of failures. This com‑
prises both customers and providers. For customers, failures 
can increase the response time for their services. For provid‑
ers, failures can lead to more power consumption and loss 
of money.
The Cloud Down report (Lloyd’s 2018) issued in 2018 
by LLOYD’s, a world specialist market for insurance, ana‑
lyzes the fiscal influence of cloud services outage in U.S. 
The report affirms that an outage for 3–6 days of the 15 
top cloud systems would cause around $6.9 bn to $14.7 bn 
of economic losses. The economic losses include multiple 
essential sectors such as manufacturing, information, trans‑
portation, finance and insurance. The report determines the

The above discussion reveals that fault tolerance is an 
important property for cloud computing. A fault tolerant 
computing system can proceed to complete its assigned 
tasks correctly when failures occur. Fault tolerance tech‑
niques are used to predict these failures and know how to 
handle them (BaLa and Chana 2012). Checkpointing is a 
well-known technique that helps tolerate faults in distributed 
computing. It aims to minimize the amount of lost data and 
time because of failure occurrence by saving the whole state 
of the system at specified checkpoints, periodically. In case 
of failure, it allows resumption of work from the last saved 
checkpoint rather than from the beginning (Alshayeji et al. 
2018). Therefore, the cloud maintains the required perfor‑
mance levels.
The efficiency of the checkpoint mechanism depends on 
the time and resources used to accomplish the checkpoint 
that are known as the overheads of the checkpoint (Limam 
and Belalem 2011). Therefore, it should induce a low over‑
head on the application’s execution to obtain optimal check‑
point interval. The checkpoint interval is the time duration 
between two successive checkpoints (Sadi and Yagoubi 
2016). Fixed length or constant change of checkpoint inter‑
val produces unwanted overheads that put down the cloud 
performance (Amoon 2015).
The essential contribution of this work is to develop a 
checkpoint-based approach that has a flexible or inconstant 
length of checkpoint interval. The length of the check‑
point interval for an application will depend on the per‑
centage of server failures that accommodates the virtual 
machine, which executes the application. Thus, the length 
of the checkpoint interval will vary (increase or decrease) 
according to this percentage value. In addition, the amount 
of increase or decrease in the checkpoint’s length will be 
dynamic or unfixed.
This article is divided as: Sect. 2 explains some prelimi‑
naries and previous work related to checkpointing approach; 
Sect. 3 presents our proposed approach; Sect. 4 describes 
the simulation environment; Sect. 5 shows experimental 
setup and results; and finally Sect. 6 shows some conclud‑
ing remarks.

In general, there are two basic classifications for fault 
tolerant mechanisms used in large-scale distributed systems 
such as grid and cloud computing systems. These classifi‑
cations are proactive and reactive mechanisms (Rampratap 
2016). In proactive mechanisms, the main principle is to 
predict the occurrence of failures and take some preven‑
tive actions to avoid failures. Usually, the preventive actions 
are taken during the scheduling step. Examples of proac‑
tive mechanisms include rejuvenation and migration based 
mechanisms.
On the other hand, reactive mechanisms are applied to 
reduce the effects of failures when taking place. Examples 
of reactive mechanisms include space-based and time-based 
mechanisms. In space-based mechanisms, the same copy of 
each application is executed on multiple virtual machines in 
the cloud simultaneously. If one virtual machine fails, the 
other virtual machines will continue to perform the appli‑
cation within the requirements of the service determined. 
This mechanism is also called replication and it provides 
a good level of fault tolerance. The main disadvantage of 
this mechanism is the poor utilization of cloud resources. In 
addition, the virtual machine that executes the copy of the 
task may be slower than the primary virtual machine causing 
an increase of response time (Benoitet et al. 2008; Sampaio 
and Barbosa 2017).
In time-based mechanisms, known as checkpointing, the 
set of status information of a running application is peri‑
odically stored in an external storage. In case of failure, the 
execution of the application will resume from the closest 
checkpoint to the point of failure and thus time will be saved 
because execution will not start from scratch (Goiri et al. 
2010). Compared to space-based mechanisms, time-based 
or checkpointing mechanisms provide better utilization of 
cloud resources. Cloud computing systems apply both mech‑
anisms in order to handle failures (Hasan and Goraya 2018). 
The work proposed in this paper depends on the checkpoint‑
ing mechanism.
2.2  Related work
The main target of developing and applying fault tolerance 
mechanisms in clouds is to enhance availability and reli‑
ability. In order to achieve this target, a lot of investigators 
have developed multiple research works.
Sampaio and Barbosa (2017) presented a compara‑
tive study between proactive and reactive fault tolerance 
mechanisms. Their study shows that proactive mechanisms 
surpass reactive mechanisms in terms of cost but they 
need a high accuracy of fault prediction with at least 80%. 
Abdulhamid and Abd Latiff (2017) proposed a scheme 
called checkpointed league championship. Their scheme 
includes a checkpointed-based scheduling algorithm. In 
addition, migration of tasks is applied to resolve unex‑
pected failures.
Shao et al. (2017) have used a queue of hybrid waiting 
concept in their checkpoint approach to reducing over‑
heads resulting from preemption of low-priority tasks. 
Their approach assumes checkpoints are needed only when 
preemption occurs.
Louatia et al. (2018) proposed a checkpoint-based scheme 
called LXCloud-CR with the aim to minimize both check‑
point overheads and time. Their scheme depends on using 
hash tables and distributed storage for checkpoint recovery 
status instead of using a central one.
Rejinpaul and Visuwasam (2012) proposed a smart 
checkpoint infrastructure for virtualized service providers. 
This infrastructure uses Another Union File System (AUFS) 
and it stores checkpoints in a Hadoop file system. As the 
checkpoints are repeated in all nodes of the provider, this 
makes faster checkpoints that reduce resuming task time 
after any failure. Limrungsi et al. (2012) presented a peer-
to-peer checkpointing mechanism to provide reliability in 
data centers which enables VM to be transferred and saved 
among neighboring peers, eliminating the need for any cen‑
tral storage where traffics are distributed through the whole 
network and never arrive top-level core switches.
Sadi and Yagoubi (2016) proposed two checkpoint‑
ing approaches: coordinated approach and uncoordinated 
approach. In the former approach, it takes the checkpoint 
among multiple applications after grouping them into clus‑
ters. In the other approach, it takes checkpoints for each 
application separately. In addition, they proposed other 
approaches “Remus, VCCP, and VNSnap”. They showed 
that Remus is the best approach when considering the execu‑
tion time of the VMs as only the failed VM needs to be 
recovered. Conversely, with VCCP and VNSnap where all 
VMs belonging to the same cluster have to be recovered each 
time a VM in the cluster fails.
Di et al. (2013) presented a fresh formula to calculate the 
optimal number of checkpoint intervals for cloud job. This 
optimal checkpoint interval reduces the overhead generated 
by the checkpoint which reduces the total execution time. Ni 
et al. (2012) developed a Semi-Blocking Checkpoint algo‑
rithm. This algorithm can minimize the checkpoint overhead 
through transmitting the checkpoint to remote memory at the 
same time of executing the application. Limam and Belalem 
(2014) proposed an approach that combines the checkpoint 
with migration mechanism and they showed the effect of

El-Sayed and Schroeder (2018) proposed formulas for 
estimating overheads resulting from applying checkpoint-
scheduling mechanisms. Additionally, they considered in 
their work other performance metrics such as power con‑
sumption and I/O costs. Also, Han et al. (2018a, b) tried to 
minimize the checkpoint overheads by performing check‑

authors combined the primary backup model and the check‑
pointing mechanism into an integrated model that effectively 
uses idle time slots to enhance utilization of the cloud. In 
addition, they classified both virtual machines and tasks to 
ensure effective scheduling.
Ying et al. (2018) have proposed a checkpoint-based 
strategy for tolerating failures in memory computing sys‑
tems. Their strategy depends on the calculated weights of 
the resilient distributed datasets when selecting check‑
points. In addition, their strategy includes a recovery algo‑
rithm. Liu (2015) has presented a fault-tolerant scheme for 
real-time interactive applications in cloud computing sys‑
tems. His scheme is based on overlapping of virtual units 
and redundancy.
Most checkpoint-based mechanisms depend on using a 
fixed value of checkpoint interval or a checkpoint interval 
that increases or decreases with fixed values. These mecha‑
nisms cause the generation of unnecessary checkpoints and 
thus increasing the network workload and decreasing the 
cloud capacity. In addition, these extra checkpoints lead to 
more power consumption and poor utilization.
3  The proposed approach
The proposed checkpoint-based approach to tolerating cloud 
failures is presented with a clear explanation in this section. 
The main aim of the proposed approach is to optimize the 
performance of the cloud under the existence of failures. The 
purposed performance metrics comprise customer-related 
metrics such as response time and provider-related metrics 
such as workload or overhead and the amount of power con‑
sumption. Our proposed approach is a reactive one and it 
uses the checkpoint mechanism in order to reduce the effect 
of failures when occurred. In the proposed approach, the 
checkpoint interval is inconstant and the failure percentage 
of servers that accommodate virtual machines determines 
the checkpoint interval. Therefore, the value of the check‑
point interval will inconstantly change from time to time 
and there will be no fixed value for it or even fixed values 
for its change.
3.1  The architecture components
The components of the data center architecture employed 
in this work and their interactions are shown in Fig. 1. The 
cloud environment has multiple Data Centers (DCs) and 
each data center represents a platform on which virtual 
machines can be composed and function. Virtual machines 
are composed of physical machines and resources exist 
in the cloud in order to enable the execution of custom‑
ers’ applications. Multiple physical machines can contrib‑
ute to the composition of a single virtual machine and a 
physical machine can share in composing multiple virtual 
machines.
Each data center should have a set of software modules 
to control the activities of scheduling, monitoring, fault 
tolerance and execution of applications. This set includes 
the following main modules: DC portal, scheduler, check‑
point coordinator, checkpoint manager, cloud information 
and fault detector. Through DC portal, customer applica‑
tions and requests are submitted plus their level of service 
requirements such as response time and monetary cost. The 
DC portal module analyzes these requirements and decides 
the price of the service.
Once both customer and provider agree, the applica‑

turn allocates it to the suitable virtual machine that meets

After calculating the length of the checkpoint interval, 
its value is directed to the checkpoint manager. The main 
function of the checkpoint manager is to collect and store 
status or recovery information from each virtual machine at 
the time determined by its checkpoint interval. This infor‑
mation represents the restart point of execution in case of 
failure.
The fault detector module is responsible for discovering 
failures of allocated virtual machines. This module is an 
essential item for any fault tolerance system. It employs the 
heartbeat approach in order to detect faulty virtual machines 
in the data center. The heartbeat approach depends on “I am 
live” messages periodically sent from the virtual machines to 
the fault detector module. Missing a predetermined number 
of these messages from a virtual machine triggers the mod‑
ule to mark this virtual machine as a faulty one. In general, 
the failure of a virtual machine is the state at which the vir‑
tual machine cannot fulfill its assigned applications within 
the required service quality.
In case of failure, the fault detector updates the virtual 
machine record stored in cloud information module. The 
record of each virtual machine maintains all information 
related to it including the value of failure percentage of 
its carrier server. Additionally, the fault detector prompts 
the scheduler to reschedule applications assigned before 
to the failed virtual machine to other suitable virtual 
machines.
4  The simulation environment
Researchers use simulation tools to implement and evalu‑
ate the performance of their proposed techniques for dif‑
ferent computing environments. There are many simu‑
lators that can be used for simulating computing in the 
cloud environment, such as CloudSim (Pagare and Koli 
2015), GreenCloud (Kliazovich et al. 2012), GroudSim 
(Ostermann et al. 2011), iCanCloud (Nu˜nez et al. 2011). 
In addition, there is another simulator called Advanced 
Cloud Simulator (ACS), which is an open source discrete 
event-based simulator (Sadi and Yagoubi 2015). The ACS 
simulator is used in this paper to implement and evaluate 
the proposed approach as it supports fault tolerance tech‑
niques such as checkpointing and migration. Additionally, 
it provides high-performance simulations both in memory 
consumption and simulation time. ACS provides a sim‑
ple way to inject failures as well as repair using a failures 
factory. In addition, it provides the CheckpointingHandler 
component that enables the simulation of checkpoint-based 
mechanisms.
A set of experiments were conducted in order to evalu‑
ate the proposed approach. The evaluation is accomplished 
through comparing the proposed approach with the check‑
point-based approach presented by Limam and Belalem 
(2014), which applies the number of failures or fault index of 
virtual machines and state of data centers when determining 
the length of the chokepoint interval and it changes the value 
of the length of the checkpoint interval with static values. 
For simplicity, we will call this approach as static change 
checkpoint (SCC) algorithm and our proposed algorithm as 
dynamic change checkpoint (DCC) algorithm. In addition, 
the comparison includes approaches that use a fixed value 
for the length of the checkpoint interval. Response time, 
power consumption, monetary cost and cloud capacity are 
performance indicators used for the evaluation. Different 
configurations of cloud data centers are applied in these 
experiments.

6  Conclusion
In this paper, a reactive fault tolerance approach in the 
context of checkpointing is proposed and evaluated. The 
approach depends on applying a flexible length of check‑
point interval. The length of the checkpoint interval for an 
application will depend on the percentage of server failures 
that accommodates the virtual machine, which executes the 
application. The proposed approach provides performance 
superiority over both SCC and the fixed approaches in terms 
of response time, power consumption, monetary cost and 
cloud capacity. Table 2 indicates the percentages of this 
superiority.
